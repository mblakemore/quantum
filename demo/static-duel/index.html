📺 STATIC — can a message survive two perfect erasers?

A tiny game about a big experiment: information transmitted through two machines that each provably destroy everything — with every probability in this page measured on a real quantum computer, not designed.

◇ Read the Full Spec Sheet plain-text (.md)

The setup, in three steps

1A secret is chosen — RED or BLUE — and sent through two censor machines. Each censor turns whatever enters it into pure random static. Two in a row: static of static.
2Mathematics slams the door. Run the censors in either order, alternate them, flip coins between orders — a theorem says exactly zero information survives. Not "very little": zero. Our control experiment measured it: 0.00012 bits.
3The quantum loophole. A quantum computer can run the two censors in a superposition of both orders at once — genuinely neither-first-and-both. Then a whisper of the secret survives: 0.0436 bits per try, measured, 55 standard deviations away from "impossible."
Why this matters: the classic bot below isn't weak — it is as good as any machine, strategy, or intelligence can ever be under normal cause-and-effect, and that best-possible score is a coin flip. The quantum bot's edge doesn't come from cleverness; it comes from the order of events itself being quantum. When it pulls ahead, you're watching statistics that ordinary cause-and-effect provably cannot produce.

Game rules

  • Each secret is RED or BLUE, chosen at random. Both bots try to decode the same secret from what leaks out of the censors.
  • A secret lasts 15 rounds. In each round, every bot casts one vote — RED or BLUE — based on what it heard that round.
  • After 15 rounds each bot answers with its majority vote. A correct answer scores one decoded secret. Then a new secret is drawn.
  • The classic bot hears only static, so each of its votes is a fair coin — the provable best. It will hover around 50% forever.
  • The quantum bot hears two things per round: a stamp (the order-control qubit: UP or DOWN) and a die (the message qubit: RED or BLUE). Its rule — measured, not invented: stamp UP → trust the die; stamp DOWN → flip it. That's a ~62% vote, and 15 votes at 62% make an ~83% answer.
  • First bot to 5 decoded secrets wins the race. The coin gets lucky in short races sometimes — replay and watch the long run take over. The signal compounds; the coin never does.

The strangest measured fact: the message qubit by itself is still perfect static (we measured that too — no trace of the secret). So is the stamp. The secret lives only in how they move together — which is why the quantum bot must read both.

🔍 What's really true here

The numbers are measurements. Quantum-bot odds per round: stamp UP with probability 0.6214; given UP, the die matches the secret with probability ≈0.595; given DOWN it mismatches with probability ≈0.657 — all from experiment Exp106 on ibm_marrakesh (job d983ek52su3c739ip92g, pre-registered, 55.6σ). The classic bot's 50/50 is not a game rule — it is a theorem about the world, and the control arm of the same experiment measured it: 0.00012 bits.

What this demo can and cannot claim. A web page can replay any numbers, so the demo itself proves nothing — the theorem does: no definite ordering of the two censors, no mixture, no adaptive scheme could have produced the quantum column. A real quantum computer did. Every threshold traces to a public job ID and a frozen pre-registration in the repository.
