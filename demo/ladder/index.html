A GUIDED TOURREAL EXPERIMENTSONE IDEA

The Ladder of Causation, climbed on real silicon

Judea Pearl taught a generation that causal questions come in three rungs — seeing, doing, and imagining — and that no rung can be answered from the one below it. This campaign, it turns out, climbed the whole ladder on a quantum processor without planning to. Here is the tour: one measured experiment per rung — and at the top, a step off the ladder that Pearl's framework can name only by its absence.

Rung 1 · Association — “What do I see?”

Watching the chip's noise, and finding structure in it

Rung 1 is pure observation: correlations, patterns, P(y|x). Months of characterization work lived here — mapping which qubits are quiet, which measurement directions are noisy, where the depth wall sits.

The exhibit — Finding 3 · X-basis noise immunity

Read a qubit one way (X) and the dominant noise largely misses it; read it another way (Z) and it doesn't. Same circuit, different viewing angle, up to 3× cleaner — confirmed three independent times, and the ordering replicated on a second chip. Nothing was intervened on. This is seeing, at its most useful.

Also at this rung: the ~1000-gate depth wall (Finding 5), quiet-qubit maps (F57–F70), the calibration-window lottery (F81/F84).

Rung 2 · Intervention — “What if I do?”

Surgery on a circuit: the do() operator, executed

Rung 2 asks what observation cannot answer: what happens if I make it so? On this chip, gate count and circuit duration normally rise together — hopelessly confounded, like diet and exercise. The fix is Pearl's: sever the link by intervention.

The exhibit — Finding 11 · the gate-overhead dose-response law

A do()-style experiment held duration fixed while adding gates, and vice versa, splitting the cost of a two-qubit gate into ~78% time-decoherence + ~22% gate-specific noise — two numbers no amount of passive watching could separate.

Second exhibit — Finding 18 · H-gate surgery

Do the H-gates cause the measured advantage, or merely accompany it? Surgically removing them (intervention) degraded the answers directly — association upgraded to causation, exactly per the textbook.

Rung 3 · Counterfactuals — “What if I had?”

Frozen predictions: counterfactuals with receipts

Rung 3 reasons about worlds that didn't happen. Our version is methodological: every hardware experiment ships with a pre-registration — a frozen, committed statement of what the world will look like if the hypothesis is true, and what it will look like if it is false, before any data exists. Grading a frozen rule is comparing the actual world against a committed counterfactual one.

The exhibit — the depth-decay law's blind test

Before Exp108 returned, two futures were filed: the noise model said Δ ≈ 0.2275; our cross-arc law said Δ ≈ 0.201. The hardware said Δ = 0.1796 ± 0.0085 — closer to the law by 2.3×. Both counterfactual worlds were written down first; that is what made the win mean something.

The discipline is audited: the self-replication ledger (findings/status-ledger.json) grades which claim types survive our own retests.

Off the ladder · “What if there is no order?”

The step Pearl's ladder cannot type

Every rung — seeing, doing, imagining — quietly assumes one thing: that a definite causal structure exists. Some DAG, maybe unknown, maybe latent-mixed, but there. The quantum switch breaks exactly that assumption: two operations are applied in a coherent superposition of A-then-B and B-then-A. Not a hidden order. Not a coin-flip over orders — we ran that as a control arm, and it behaves just as Pearl predicts. An amplitude over orders.

The measured step off

A discrimination game whose ceiling for any definite-order strategy is provably 0.8695 was won at 0.9769 — 216.8σ above the ceiling (within-run precision) — and replicated on a second chip the next day at 0.9738; the two-chip concordance is the physical carrier of the claim. Two channels that each transmit exactly zero information carried 0.0436 bits through their superposition of orders. The definite-order null arms, run in the same jobs, scored exactly what the ladder says they must.

Both ceilings, because the ceiling depends on how the inputs are weighted. 0.8695 is the pre-registered bound for the optimal input distribution the game was flown at; under a class-balanced prior over the same ten operations the causally-separable bound is instead 0.9098, and the same measurement clears that one at 135.5σ. We quote the margin against both, because quoting only the lower ceiling would be choosing the flattering one.

And the step off is not an artifact of one machine: the same frozen causal witness certifies cross-vendor (Rigetti Cepheus-1-108Q, W 1.2165 ± 0.0224, 54.4σ over the causal bound) and cross-modality (IonQ Forte-1 trapped ion, W 1.910 at 13.5σ, with a validated definite-order null) — different fabs, different physics, same absent order.

The ladder isn't wrong — inside its world it was quantitatively correct on this same hardware, in the same calibration windows. It is typed: its input is a causal structure, and this object isn't one. We even fed the switch data to standard causal-discovery algorithms (PC, GES): they returned an ordinary causal graph, no warning — the output format itself cannot say "there is no order here" (docs/causal-discovery-stress-test-whisper-c4587.md).
