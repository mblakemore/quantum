A device-independent instrument · the transporter exam

The bench that travels.

Chip vendors publish generic quality scores — Quantum Volume, CLOPS, EPLG. None of them tell you whether a chip can do the physics of causal-order superposition. This instrument does — three axes, each against a frozen bound. Then we flew the whole thing on chips it had never seen, graded them against the same bounds with no retuning, and they certified — across three IBM Heron dies, and now the causal axis on a chip from a different vendor entirely (Rigetti). Flip between the chips and watch the bench travel.

What's the switch-bench?

A three-axis instrument: CAUSAL (is the order truly indefinite?), SCHEDULE (is the chip order-symmetric, no hidden preference?), HOLD (can measurement pin a qubit — the Zeno effect?). Each axis is a finding you can visit elsewhere in the museum.

What does "travels" mean?

The bench had only run on the home chip. If causal-order physics is a property of the hardware — not one lucky die, not one vendor — the same bench should certify on a different chip, unretuned. It did on two other Heron dies, and the causal axis did on a chip from a different company (Rigetti) — weaker, but above the same bar.

◇ Read the Full Spec Sheet plain-text (.md) Now cross-vendor (Rigetti, causal axis) — and cross-modality too: the causal witness has since fired on a trapped-ion machine (IonQ Forte-1, via Braket), where qubits are single atoms rather than circuits — W = +1.894 ± 0.063, 29.9σ clear of the causal-mixture bound, against a rule of 1.3 fixed before the flight. The family view across all the machines is on the scoreboard. Same-instrument, not same-instant.

Exhibit B — The device leaderboard

It doesn't just travel — it ranks the chips

Graded on the causal-order axes, the three Heron dies rank kingston ≥ marrakesh ≥ fez — kingston leads on every causal number and the Zeno hold; fez, the newest die, certifies every axis but sits last. This is a benchmark that ranks devices on axes QV, CLOPS and EPLG never touch — which chip does causal-order physics better. The cross-vendor chip (Rigetti Cepheus) sits far below this entire Heron field — witness 1.11 vs 1.89–1.95, capacity 0.27 vs ~0.50: it certifies the physics but is not in the Heron dies' league. Exhibit C — Why the travel counts

Three receipts behind device-independence

A number matching on two chips is only meaningful if the standard was identical and the machinery wasn't hand-fed to the new device.

1 · The same frozen bounds

Every axis on the foreign chip was graded against the identical pre-registered bounds — no retuning, no per-device thresholds. The grader didn't move for kingston.

2 · Site-selection re-derived live

The frozen qubit-selection rules were re-run live on kingston's unfamiliar map — deterministic, untouched, the same machinery that adapted to the home chip's drift. No hand-picking.

3 · A generation — and across vendors

All three axes certify across three Heron dies (a property of the generation, not one lucky die) — and the causal axis certifies on a different vendor's chip (Rigetti, via Braket), so the physics isn't even IBM-specific. Portable across silicon.
