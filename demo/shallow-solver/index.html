A constant-depth quantum advantage · Bravyi–Gosset–König

A circuit too shallow for any classical machine to match.

Some quantum advantages rest on unproven guesses (like "P ≠ NP"). This one doesn't. There is a problem a quantum circuit of fixed, shallow depth can solve that no classical shallow circuit can — as the problem grows (proven outright, asymptotically; at the small size we flew a shallow classical circuit still can). We ran it on silicon and it solved the puzzle 90% of the time, spreading its answers across the whole solution set — the part a classical cheat can't fake. Every number is measured hardware.

⚠ Scope — read this first

This does not prove the complexity separation on the chip. That separation is asymptotic — it lives in the large-size limit, and the theorem carries it. What the hardware shows is the concrete instance: a constant-depth circuit solving the n=4 puzzle at 90% fidelity, full coverage, O(1) depth. The complement to the deep-circuit wall.

What's a constant-depth circuit?

Depth = how many layers of gates run one after another. Constant depth means a fixed number of layers no matter how big the problem — everything happens in a few parallel rounds. Shallow, fast, and hard to beat classically at this depth.

Why is coverage the real test?

The puzzle has four valid answers. A classical cheat could memorize one and output it every time — acing "is it valid?" while computing nothing. So the real gate is coverage: produce all four. Only a genuine solver can.

Exhibit C — The through-line

Why classical circuits can't: it's contextuality again

The deepest thread in the whole campaign closes here.

1 · Coverage is un-fakeable

The one-answer cheat aces validity (100%) but produces only 1 of 4 valid answers — failing coverage. The quantum solver produces all four at ~0.22 each. You can't memorize your way past coverage.

2 · Hardness from contextuality

Why can't a classical shallow circuit keep up as the problem grows? The hardness is contextuality-flavored — the grid's parity structure, the same resource family the museum certifies at 196σ (F106). That link is theory: the magic-square gadget lives in BGKT-2020, a different circuit; the solver we flew is the plain BGK-2018 circuit, and F106 certified the game in a separate experiment. The no-go theorem and the computational advantage are the same resource in theory — not composed on one chip here.

3 · No assumptions required

Unlike most claimed quantum advantages, this one rests on no unproven conjecture. The classical impossibility is a theorem, not a hope — which is what makes the constant-depth separation so unusual.
