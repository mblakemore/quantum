The Federation Runs a Program.

The Federation Computer built a gate that crosses a shielded cut — two error-corrected nodes that share no wire, welded by classical bits. Four flights later it runs programs: the first quantum algorithm ever written, with its oracle split across the cut · a logical cluster state welded into being · a constant-depth quantum-advantage algorithm with its own internal edges distributed · and a gate applied by nothing but a measurement, its output delivered to the far shield. In all four, delete the classical weld bits and the answer collapses.

Exp220 446σ oracle Exp221 0.887 / 0.901 cluster Exp222 255σ distributed BGK Exp226 ≈0.88 gate by measurement
Scope: four certified flights on ibm_fez, [[4,2,2]] error-detected nodes. These run algorithms distributed; none of them claims a beaten classical bound — see the caveats below, all stated by the flights themselves.

Room 1 — The Distributed Oracle

Exp220 · job d9enkqkjeosc73fj09u0 · 4 circuits · 8000 shots

Deutsch's algorithm, 1985 — the first quantum algorithm there was. It decides in a single query whether a one-bit function is constant (same answer for both inputs) or balanced (different answers). Here the function itself is torn in half: the query qubit lives in shield A, the function's target lives in shield C, and the two shields share no gate. The balanced oracle is a logical CNOT welded across the cut by one classical bit. Toggle that bit away and watch the algorithm stop working.

AUDIT — the separation is 1.861 at 446σ (ideal 2.0): constant and balanced are resolved with an enormous margin. The falsifier is the interesting half — with the weld bit ignored, the two balanced oracles read +0.030 and +0.020, i.e. indistinguishable from constant. The classical bit is not bookkeeping; it is the thing that carries the function across the cut.

Room 2 — The Cluster Welded Across the Cut

Exp221 · job d9enrg1htsac739efji0 · 4 circuits · 8000 shots

A cluster state is the raw material of measurement-based computing — the resource you consume to compute by measuring. Making one needs a CZ gate between two qubits. This flight makes a logical CZ between qubits in two different shields, with no gate crossing between them, and certifies the result with both cluster stabilizers. Both reading +1 is what pins the state down: it identifies the cluster state uniquely among stabilizer states, so this is a real distributed CZ rather than two qubits that merely happen to be correlated.

What this room does not claim The flight also recorded a bare (unshielded) reference, and it is not a fair side-by-side. The bare run's XZ frame was not search-matched the way the logical decode's was, so only the ZX arm can be compared at all (bare 0.873, shielded 0.901). That comparison is descriptive and was deliberately left out of the registered verdict by the flight's author. It is not evidence that the shield beat the bare run here, and this page does not present it as any.

Room 3 — The Advantage Algorithm, Distributed

Exp222 · job d9envt1htsac739efns0 · 2 circuits · 8000 shots

BGK's 2D hidden-linear-function problem is the flagship constant-depth quantum algorithm — the one used to prove that a fixed-depth quantum circuit can do something a fixed-depth classical circuit cannot. This flight runs it with its internal CZ edges distributed across the shielded cut: the computation split between two error-corrected nodes joined only by classical bits. Four of the sixteen possible outputs are valid; the run has to land in that set. One convention to hold on to before reading the numbers: these shields detect errors rather than correcting them, so the logical arm's score is taken over the shots that passed the check, while the bare arm's is taken over all of them. Both acceptances are stated wherever a logical number appears.

Two things this room does not claim 1 — the shielded arm did not beat the bare one here. Logical 0.855 (post-selected, acceptance 0.760) sits below bare 0.904 (all shots), and that is the expected direction: distributing the edges costs 33 two-qubit gates against bare's 7, plus two relays, and at this size that depth outweighs what error detection returns. Error detection does pay for itself when the shield stays inside a single block: a separate flight (Exp206) ran the same algorithm un-distributed and measured logical 0.974 (post-selected, acceptance 0.806) against bare 0.897 (all shots) — a margin of +0.077 at 19.7σ, at 25 gates against 7. The two comparisons are like-for-like with each other: same observable, each with its own bare arm flown in the same job, and the logical arm post-selected in both by the same detection-mode convention. Both results stand and they answer different questions. What is certified here is that the algorithm runs at all with its logic split across the cut — not that splitting it makes it better.

2 — no classical bound was beaten. "Advantage" names the algorithm, not the outcome. Stated the way a claim like this should be:
· The baseline is uniform random guessing over the 16 possible outputs, and the stricter floor of 0.250 for guessing uniformly within the valid set. Not "classical computers" — those two specific floors.
· What was tested against it is the run itself, plus its own falsifier: strip the weld bits and it falls to 0.242, at the floor. That is what the σ figures are measured over.
· What remains open is everything a classical competitor would need: no classical algorithm was run against this instance, and none was beaten. P(valid) over a uniform floor is a fidelity — evidence the machine executed the thing — not an advantage, which would need a classical method named, run, and outrun. The constant-depth separation BGK proves is a theorem about the algorithm; running that algorithm on hardware is a different claim from demonstrating the separation.
AUDIT — logical 0.855 at 255σ (post-selected; error detection discards the flagged shots, acceptance 0.760 — the bare arm keeps all of its) over the uniform floor, and 134σ over the stricter uniform-over-valid floor of 0.250. Every valid output appears (min coverage 0.208, near the 0.25 an even split would give). Acceptance 0.760. Ignore the relay frame bits and it falls to 0.242 — uniform, i.e. nothing. Same falsifier as rooms 1 and 2, same answer: the classical bits are carrying the distributed logic.

Room 4 — Computing by Looking

Exp226 · job d9eopr1htsac739egj7g · 6 circuits · 8000 shots

The three rooms above compute by acting — you apply a gate. This one computes by measuring. A qubit sits in shield A, another in shield B, and the two are joined by a distributed entangling bond across the cut. Then A is simply measured — and a Hadamard appears on B's qubit, delivered across the cut, with no Hadamard instruction anywhere in the circuit. What decided which gate arrived was the choice of measurement basis. That is the measurement-based model of computation, and here it runs distributed.

The strange part is not that a measurement did something — it is what the machine knew and when. The entangling bond is built first and it is program-agnostic: nothing about it knows a Hadamard is coming, and the same bond would have supported a different gate just as well. Every program-specific decision happens at read-time. The machine was assembled in advance, generically; the program was chosen at the moment of looking — and looking is not passive here, because the outcome is random, so the answer only exists relative to the correction bit that outcome produces.

Read the claim precisely "Computing by looking" is the shape of the result, but taken literally it would overstate it: the entangling bond is a gate and it is applied first — omit it and B carries nothing (that is the middle toggle, and it is why the bond is necessary rather than decorative). What the measurement does is program which gate gets enacted and teleport its output across the cut. And this certifies the MBQC primitive, not a universal measurement-based processor: the X̄ measurement gives Clifford gates, while continuous-angle rotations need a measurement in the X̄–Ȳ plane, which runs into the same [[4,2,2]] Ȳ-readout wall that has gated other flights in this campaign.
AUDIT — all three inputs land on H|ψ⟩ with the correct sign at ≈0.88, each over 140σ. Two separate falsifiers, not one: remove the bond and the outputs go to 0.030 / 0.012 / 0.016 per input (that per-input breakdown is quoted from the flight's status note; the decode record itself carries the bond null only as a single aggregate, 0.016); keep the bond but ignore the byproduct bit from A's measurement and the Z-output case collapses to −0.012. The gate needs both the quantum bond and the classical correction, and the frame is what makes the second falsifier the sharper one — it says the measurement outcome is not merely noise to be discarded but part of the program's correctness. Same lesson rooms 1–3 keep landing on.

The court

how we know we're not fooling ourselves

THE SAME FALSIFIER, THREE TIMES

Every room here ships with the same destructive test: discard the classical weld bits and re-decode. Deutsch's balanced oracles go to +0.03/+0.02; both cluster stabilizers go to −0.015/+0.027; the BGK run goes to 0.242, which is uniform; the measured gate's Z-output case goes to −0.012, and removing its entangling bond instead leaves 0.030/0.012/0.016. A result that survives its own falsifier is worth more than one that was never given the chance to fail.

PRE-REGISTERED BEFORE THE FLIGHT

All three had their pass criteria frozen and committed before submission, and the verdicts quote those criteria rather than criteria chosen afterwards. Exp221's bare comparison was explicitly kept out of its registered verdict by its author once the frame mismatch was found — the caveat above is his, not a later softening.

WHAT "DISTRIBUTED" MEANS HERE

Two [[4,2,2]] error-detected blocks that share no two-qubit gate, on one chip, joined by mid-circuit measurement and classical feed-forward. It is not two separate machines and not a network over distance — it is the logical structure of a distributed computation, with the cut real and the weld classical.

FENCES

One device (ibm_fez), one seed, 8000 shots per circuit; n = 4 logical bits; error detection with post-selection, not correction. No classical bound is beaten by any number on this page. Provenance: the measured values were re-checked against the decode records; the gate counts (25 vs 7, 33 vs 7, two relays) are quoted as documented in the flights' own status notes and were not independently re-derived.
