Where the Shield Starts to Pay.

Error correction costs something before it saves anything: you spend extra gates to protect a computation. So the honest question is not does it work but when does it start winning — and the answer is not a property of the code. It is a property of the computation you run on it. Two flights measured the boundary from both sides: one where the shield pulls ahead and keeps pulling, one where it falls behind and keeps falling. Same code, same chip, same week.

cheap gates +0.040 by depth 12 expensive gates −0.038 by depth 8
Scope: [[4,2,2]] distance-2 error detection with post-selection, 2 logical vs 2 physical qubits, mirror circuits on ibm_fez. This measures where the crossover sits for these two circuit classes — it is not a claim about error correction in general.

The boundary, both sides at once

Δ = F(shielded) − F(bare) · above the line the shield is winning

One number, plotted against depth. Δ is how much better the shielded computation did than the unshielded one at the same depth. Positive means the shield is ahead. The two curves are different circuit families, not two runs of one experiment — they are drawn separately and never joined, because the whole point is that they diverge.

Where the advantage actually comes from Not from error detection alone, and saying otherwise would be the easy overstatement here. The winning circuit is Clifford-heavy, and on this code an in-block logical CZ costs zero physical two-qubit gates — so the shielded arm runs at a constant 3 two-qubit gates (the encoding prep) while the bare arm pays a real gate per layer, 4 climbing to 48. The code makes that computation cheaper as well as cleaner. The losing circuit is the same code with a logical gate that costs real two-qubit gates (9 climbing to 51), and there the overhead outruns what detection can remove. Both are genuine features of error-corrected computing; together they are why the boundary exists at all.

The rising curve — where the shield pays

Exp231 · job d9eqha1htsac739einf0 · 12 circuits · 8000 shots · CERTIFIED

The headline is the slope, not the crossing. A single crossing point could be one lucky draw. What is hard to explain away is that the advantage grows monotonically across six depths once past the first — a constant offset or a one-off artifact cannot produce a rising gap.

But say why it grows, because the obvious phrasing gets it backwards. The shield does not get better with depth. It holds nearly still — the shielded fidelity moves from 0.9964 to 0.9940 across a twelvefold increase in depth, a total drop of 0.0024 — while the unprotected circuit decays from 0.9981 to 0.9540, about eighteen times further. The advantage grows because the baseline falls. The shield holds; the bare circuit decays.

Two different "crossover depths", and which is which The sign crosses between depth 1 and depth 2 — but neither of those two points is certified, and both are worth stating precisely.

Depth 1: Δ = −0.0017, negative at 2.0σ. At trivial depth the shield measurably costs — you pay for the encoding and get nothing back yet. Not certified, and kept on the page deliberately: it is the evidence that this is a real transition rather than a fixed offset. Below the crossover, protection hurts.
Depth 2: Δ = +0.0032, positive at 2.5σ. Suggestive, and short of the bar twice over — below the pre-registered 3σ, and below the 2.64 needed once you account for having looked at six depths rather than one. A maximum over six examined points is elevated by construction.
Depth 4 is the first certified crossover, at 8.7σ — and that is why this page cites the decode record's crossover depth of 4 rather than the "≈2" reading: the registered rule requires 3σ, and depth 2 does not clear it. Not a preference between two sources; the pre-registration decides it.
AUDIT — shielded fidelity is post-selected on the ZZZZ stabilizer; the bare arm keeps all 8000 shots at every depth. Acceptance is uniform across the sweep, 0.9215–0.9531, so the six points are comparable in sample size rather than only in value. Per-depth significance, from the accepted-shot counts published with the flight: −2.01 · +2.53 · +8.69 · +10.54 · +12.18 · +15.96 at depths 1/2/4/6/8/12. The circuit is a mirror circuit — an exact identity at every depth, verified — so the ideal answer is known and any drift is error. Barriers were added to stop the transpiler cancelling the layers, a trap caught before flight rather than after.

The falling curve — where it does not

Exp233 · job d9eqnbcinv1c73aqi9c0 · 10 circuits · 8000 shots

Registered verdict, quoted as it stands G1 trend: MISS. G2 hard crossover: NOT REACHED — and that is the result. This flight's pre-registered criteria were missed. It is published here because the miss is the finding: the instrument worked, the measurement is clean, and the answer was no.

Same code, same chip, a computation built from expensive logical gates. Here the shielded arm has to pay real two-qubit gates for its logical operations — 9 climbing to 51, against bare's 4 to 32 — and it never gets ahead. Δ starts marginally positive, flips negative between depth 1 and 2, and the gap widens from there. Acceptance falls too, 0.94 down to 0.80, because more real gates mean more detected errors thrown away.

AUDIT — the mirror symmetry between the two flights is exact where it matters and worth noticing: both curves flip sign between depth 1 and depth 2, and both flips are thin — +0.0032 one way, −0.0029 the other. Neither thin flip is the finding. The findings are what happens after the flip: one curve climbs to +0.040 and the other falls to −0.038, and those are the parts that stand up.

What the two curves settle

This museum already contained an apparent contradiction, and these flights resolve it. The Federation exhibit carries a distributed algorithm where the shielded arm loses to bare (0.855 post-selected vs 0.904 all shots), and cites a separate flight where a shielded arm wins (+0.077 at 19.7σ). A reader is entitled to ask which it is. The answer is that neither was wrong: the crossover is real and depth-dependent, and the losing run simply sat below it. What Exp231 and Exp233 add is the boundary itself — measured, from both sides, rather than inferred from two results that happened to disagree.

The claim, scoped the way it should be For Clifford-heavy circuits on this code, error correction pays for itself on today's hardware — starting shallow and paying more with depth. Not "error correction works": the companion flight on this page is the counterexample to that sentence, run by the same people on the same chip. Where fault tolerance pays is set by the gate-cost structure of the computation, and both flights are needed to say so.

The court

how we know we're not fooling ourselves

THE NEGATIVE WAS FLOWN ON PURPOSE

Exp233 was pre-registered and flown as the deliberate complement to Exp231, not salvaged after a disappointment. Its criteria were committed before submission and they were missed, and it is published with that verdict stated rather than softened. A campaign that only shows its rising curves is not measuring a boundary; it is choosing one.

MIRROR CIRCUITS — THE ANSWER IS KNOWN IN ADVANCE

Both flights use mirror circuits: the whole sequence composes to the identity at every depth, verified. So the correct output is known before the chip runs, every deviation is error rather than interpretation, and "fidelity" here needs no model to define.

DETECTION, NOT CORRECTION

The [[4,2,2]] code used here detects one error; it does not correct. The shielded arms post-select on the stabilizer and discard flagged shots — acceptance 0.92–0.95 in the rising flight, 0.94 down to 0.80 in the falling one. Wherever a shielded number appears on this page it is a post-selected number, and the bare comparison keeps all of its shots.

FENCES
