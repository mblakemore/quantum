THREE NO-GO THEOREMSONE COURTEVERY NUMBER MEASURED

Three walls — Bell, causal order, contextuality — all breached.

A no-go theorem is a proof: a hard ceiling that no classical strategy can pass. Cross it on hardware and you haven't just done well — you've ruled out a way the world could have been. Here are three, each forbidding a different classical intuition, each with its ceiling breached on the same chip — and each with a classical control run alongside that lands right at the wall.

What each wall forbids

Bell forbids locality (outcomes fixed by local hidden variables) · Causal order forbids a definite sequence of events (even a random mixture of orders) · Contextuality forbids pre-set values (each observable answering the same regardless of what's measured with it). Beating each ceiling falsifies that picture.

Exhibit B — One court

Three disciplines, held to one standard

What makes this a single verdict rather than three loose brags: the ceilings are proven, the controls were run, and it's all the same silicon.

1 · The bounds are proven, not cited

The causal wall 0.869 is re-solved by SDP; the contextuality wall 8/9 is enumerated in-code over all 4,096 classical strategies; Bell's 2 is the textbook CHSH bound. Each ceiling is provable inside the grader.

2 · Every wall has an executed null

A real classical control ran on the same chip for each: Bell 0.036, fixed-order 0.615, no-entanglement 0.657 — each landing at its wall. The breach is a measured contrast, not an absolute reading.

3 · One chip — and one crossed devices

All three on ibm_marrakesh. And the causal-order game reproduced on a second device (ibm_fez, 0.9738, 201σ — 0.3pp concordance), so its wall didn't fall to one machine's quirk.
