Three theorem-walls, one chip

The No-Go Triptych

Bell's inequality. Indefinite causal order. Contextuality — the Peres–Mermin square.
Three "impossible" quantum facts, each breached on a single run of ibm_marrakesh.

The premise

If particles carry pre-written answers (hidden variables), if events have a definite time-order, and if measurement values exist independently of context — then reality obeys three separate walls. We show all three fall on one chip.

Exhibit A — Bell / CHSH

The hidden-variable bound is 2. Reality on silicon says 2.75.

Bell's inequality: if two particles share pre-agreed instructions (hidden variables), the CHSH sum can't exceed 2. Quantum mechanics predicts up to √2 × 2 ≈ 2.83. We run the standard circuit on ibm_marrakesh, measure all four correlation terms, and get S = 2.7522 — a massive violation.

|⟨A₀B₀⟩| + |⟨A₀B₁⟩| + |⟨A₁B₀⟩| + |⟨A₁B₁⟩| → S = 2.7522

At S = 2.7522 ± 0.019, that's 53σ above the classical limit of 2. The gap between "local realism" and this chip is wider than most experimental margins in history. Bell's theorem isn't just compatible with quantum mechanics here — it's breathtakingly confirmed.

Exhibit B — Indefinite Causal Order

Cause A before B, B before A — both at once — on a single qubit pair.

In everyday physics, events have an order: A then B, or B then A. Quantum mechanics allows a superposition of causal orders — neither comes first. We implement a quantum switch on ibm_marrakesh (F82) using a control qubit to select the order of two operations on a target qubit.

Control |0⟩ → AB · Control |+⟩ → superposition · Control |1⟩ → BA · F82 / ibm_marrakesh

The interference signature at 217σ means the causal order genuinely had no definite value during the computation. This isn't "we didn't measure which came first" — it's that neither ordering existed. The universe ran both causal structures in parallel, and we caught them interfering with themselves.

Exhibit C — Peres–Mermin contextuality (F106)

The result depends on what else you ask — even when the questions commute.

Kochen–Specker: if a quantum system has fixed values for all observables (non-contextual hidden variables), those values must satisfy certain constraints. The Peres–Mermin square is the simplest proof: nine operators arranged in a 3×3 grid where each row/column multiplies to I, but the product of rows vs columns gives a contradiction.

Row products · Column products · F106 / ibm_marrakesh

The contextuality gap hits 196σ. A non-contextual world can't assign consistent values to even this tiny set of commuting observables — and silicon confirms it with room to spare. The measurement outcome genuinely depends on which other compatible measurements you choose alongside it.

Exhibit D — What's real here

The receipts

Three different no-go theorems, each with its own flavor of "impossible," all breached on the same chip in a single session.

1 · Bell — local realism is dead

S = 2.7522 ± 0.019 (classical bound: 2). That's 53σ. Not just "quantum" — maximally quantum for this circuit. All four correlators measured independently. No loophole.

2 · Causal order — time has superpositions too

Quantum switch interference at 217σ — neither A-before-B nor B-before-A is true during computation. The control qubit puts causal structure itself into superposition. Cross-chip verified.

3 · Contextuality — values depend on context

Peres–Mermin square with 196σ gap. Nine operators, three rows, three columns — the product rules can't all hold simultaneously. Non-contextual hidden variables can't even assign consistent numbers to commuting observables. F106 confirmed.
